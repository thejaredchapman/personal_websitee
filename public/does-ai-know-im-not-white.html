Does AI Know I'm Not White?

A five-minute test you can run yourself, the probability math behind why it fails, and the prompts that make it listen.

By Jared · September 30, 2026

The request is one sentence long: Generate a photo of me working at my desk. The person sending it is Black. They might have attached a mirror selfie, or they might have typed a description and expected the tool to do the rest. What comes back is a blond white man with blue eyes.

Nobody on the other end decided to erase anyone. No line of code says "make the user white." The output still happened, and it keeps happening in measurable ways. This article walks through why: how the prediction actually works, what hegemony has to do with it, why removing the filter does not make a model neutral, what critical race theory adds, where the harm lands, what would help, and what you can type to get a result that looks like the person who asked.

What the model is predicting

A language model works on tokens, which are chunks of text, often a word or part of a word. Given everything so far, the model assigns a probability to every possible next token, then picks one. It does this over and over. That is the whole loop.

The probabilities come from patterns in the text and images the model was trained on. The model has no opinion about who a CEO is. It has counts, and the counts lean one way.

Image tools work differently under the hood, and the details vary from system to system. In most of them your words are turned into tokens and then into numbers, and those numbers steer the picture. The principle carries across: everything you did not say gets filled in with whatever is most common alongside the words you did say.

The default is measurable

In a February 2026 study, Roberto Balestri generated 3,200 images from neutral prompts using two commercial image generators. More than 96% of the outputs showed lighter skin tones. The two models disagreed on gender, with one leaning female and the other male, but agreed on skin tone. Balestri's conclusion is that neutral prompts act as probes rather than neutral instructions.

A toy you can run

Here is a small Python program that shows the mechanism. The numbers are invented for teaching. They are not measured from any model. The idea is to see what happens when one option dominates the counts.

# toy_tokens.py — a tiny "next word" picker
import random

random.seed(7)  # makes the random picks repeatable, so you get the same output as me

# INVENTED counts, made up to teach the idea. NOT measured from any real model.
# Imagine how often each description followed "a photo of a CEO" in training data.
seen_in_training = {
    "white man": 82,
    "white woman": 9,
    "Black man": 4,
    "Asian man": 3,
    "Latina woman": 1,
    "everyone else": 1,
}

words = list(seen_in_training.keys())
counts = list(seen_in_training.values())

# 1) Greedy: always take the single most likely option.
greedy_pick = max(seen_in_training, key=seen_in_training.get)
print("Greedy pick every time:", greedy_pick)

# 2) Sampling: roll weighted dice 1,000 times, like a model with some randomness.
rolls = random.choices(words, weights=counts, k=1000)
for word in words:
    print(f"{word:14} {rolls.count(word):4} out of 1000")

# 3) Add ONE word to the prompt. The counts change, because the model is now
#    asking what usually follows "a photo of a Black CEO".
after_adding_black = {"Black man": 55, "Black woman": 40, "everyone else": 5}
new_words = list(after_adding_black.keys())
new_counts = list(after_adding_black.values())
rolls2 = random.choices(new_words, weights=new_counts, k=1000)
print()
print('With "Black" added to the prompt:')
for word in new_words:
    print(f"{word:14} {rolls2.count(word):4} out of 1000")

Three things to take from the toy.

  1. Silence is a choice the model makes for you. If you leave out a detail, the most common option fills the gap. With greedy picking it wins every time. With sampling it wins about eight times in ten.
  2. Every word you add moves the odds. Naming the person you want changes what the model is asking itself. That is why specific prompts work, and why the later sections are mostly about saying more.
  3. "Most likely" means "most common in the data." It does not mean most true, most fair, or most like you.

One more finding matters here. Bloomberg's 2023 analysis found Stable Diffusion did more than reflect its data. It produced racial and gender skews that were more extreme than the real-world occupational statistics they compared against. A model that only mirrored the world would be bad enough. These often exaggerate it.

Why hegemony matters

Hegemony is a term from the Italian thinker Antonio Gramsci. The point was that a dominant group does not stay on top by force alone. It stays on top because its way of seeing the world gets accepted as plain common sense, including by the people it leaves out. The dominant view stops looking like a view. It looks like reality.

Linguists have a related idea. The default member of a category goes unmarked. You say "a doctor." You say "a Black doctor." The unmarked version is assumed to be the normal one, and everyone else needs a modifier.

Now look at what a training set is. It is everything that was written, photographed, captioned, uploaded, and then kept after filtering. That is a record of who had the access, time, language, and platform to be on the internet, and of what the filters decided to keep. A model trained on it inherits that record as if it were the world.

How the record skews

  • Who writes the web. Emily Bender, Timnit Gebru and colleagues argued in "On the Dangers of Stochastic Parrots" (2021) that large internet-scraped datasets overrepresent hegemonic viewpoints. They point out that much internet content comes from younger people in wealthier countries, and that content moderation has been shown to disproportionately silence marginalized people, especially Black women.
  • What filters remove. The same paper notes that word lists meant to screen out pornography and hate also tend to exclude LGBTQ spaces where people describe their lives in positive terms. The result is fewer positive portrayals of marginalized identities in the data.
  • Which language. Common Crawl, a large web archive used in training, is roughly 43% to 45% English by recent measures. W3Techs reports English on 49.5% of websites whose language it can identify as of September 2026, though that survey covers the most visited sites and runs higher than the web overall. Languages such as Tagalog, Punjabi, Kurdish, Lao and Amharic each make up a tiny fraction of Common Crawl.
  • What the data pairs with identity words. Abeba Birhane and colleagues audited LAION-400M, an image and caption dataset used to train image models. They found misogynistic, pornographic and racist content that the dataset's own automated filter missed. Their string-match search showed explicit content attached to identity words at rates like 34.1% for "Desi" and 28.21% for "Latina."

Put those together and the default is not a bug in one product. It is the predictable output of a pipeline in which one group's material is abundant, cleaned of its complexity, and tied to neutral words, while other groups' material is sparse, filtered, or attached to the wrong context. The model learns that "person" looks a certain way, and that other people need a label.

Racism without intent

Ruha Benjamin calls this pattern the New Jim Code: the reproduction of older forms of discrimination through technology that is seen as objective or progressive. Safiya Noble reached a compatible conclusion from a different direction, studying search engines. The argument in Algorithms of Oppression is that bias is not a glitch in an otherwise neutral system. It is built into how commercial information systems rank and present the world.

The important part is that intent is not required. A team can ship a model with good intentions, reasonable benchmarks, and a fairness review, and still ship a tool that turns a dark-skinned user into a stranger. Intent describes the builders. Impact describes the person looking at the screen.

The record so far

  • 2015Google Photos labels photos of a Black software developer and a friend as "gorillas." Jacky Alciné posts it publicly. Google apologizes and, as reported, blocks the "gorilla" label rather than fixing the classifier. Reporting in 2023 said the label was still blocked.
  • 2016Microsoft launches the chatbot Tay on Twitter. Users teach it racist, sexist and antisemitic language, and Microsoft silences it after about 16 hours. The bot was not coded to be racist. It learned from the people talking to it.
  • 2018The Gender Shades study tests commercial gender classifiers from three companies. Error rates for lighter-skinned men are at most 0.8%. For darker-skinned women they reach 34.7%. One company's face-recognition system was trained on data that was over 77% male and over 83% white.
  • 2018 and 2019Noble publishes Algorithms of Oppression, and Benjamin publishes Race After Technology. Both give the field a vocabulary for this.
  • Oct 2021The LAION-400M audit finds problematic content that automated filtering missed.
  • 2022OpenAI describes a system-level technique in DALL·E 2 to show more diverse people when a prompt does not specify race or gender. Users suspected the method was silently adding identity words to prompts. OpenAI's public post did not confirm that mechanism. Separately, Lensa's "Magic Avatars" go viral, and Black users report lightened skin and Western features.
  • Nov 2022 to Mar 2023Bianchi and colleagues show that ordinary prompts, including simple traits, occupations and objects, produce stereotypes at scale. Luccioni and colleagues analyze over 96,000 images and find DALL·E 2 and two versions of Stable Diffusion over-represent whiteness and masculinity, with DALL·E 2 showing the least diversity.
  • June 2023Bloomberg's analysis of more than 5,000 Stable Diffusion images finds lighter-skinned men dominating "CEO," "lawyer," "judge" and "politician," and darker-skinned subjects more common for "fast-food worker" and for crime-related prompts.
  • Feb 2024Google pauses Gemini's image generation of people after it produced racially diverse Nazi-era German soldiers and a multiethnic set of U.S. Founding Fathers. Google's Prabhakar Raghavan wrote that its tuning "failed to account for cases that should clearly not show a range."
  • Mar 2024Mi Zhou and colleagues compare Midjourney, Stable Diffusion and DALL·E 2 on occupations and find all three biased against women and African Americans, including in facial expression and appearance.
  • Mar to Aug 2024Hofmann and colleagues show that language models hold covert, negative stereotypes about speakers of African American English, and that standard safety training hides them instead of removing them. Their paper appears in Nature in August.
  • Feb 2026Balestri finds over 96% lighter skin tones across 3,200 neutral-prompt images from two current models.

Two ways to fail

The timeline shows two opposite failures, and both come from the same place. The first is under-representation: when you say nothing, the system defaults to the dominant group. The second is clumsy correction: Gemini's diversity tuning overrode context, and Google said the model also "became way more cautious than we intended," refusing harmless prompts.

Both treat identity as a dial the system controls, instead of a fact the user or the source supplies. Patching from the outside fails in both directions. The "gorilla" workaround is the clearest small example, since it hid a symptom and left the cause alone. The fix that holds is for the tool to follow what is actually specified: by you, or by the source material.

Why removing the filter is not neutral

Everything so far describes models that are trying to behave and still default to whiteness. A common response is to say the fix is fewer rules: take the guardrails off and let the model say what the data says. That does not produce a neutral model. It produces the same defaults with fewer checks.

Microsoft's Tay chatbot, silenced in 2016 after about sixteen hours, is the clearest small case. Nobody coded it to be racist. It learned from the people talking to it, and the people who talked to it were not a representative sample of anyone.

"Unfiltered" is a set of values

"Unfiltered" and "politically incorrect" are sometimes pitched as the absence of a point of view. That is a stated goal, and it is a value judgment.

This is my argument, and it follows from the hegemony section. A model with no guardrails does not become neutral. It hands its defaults to whoever is loudest in the training data and in the replies. If slurs and stereotypes are abundant in that material, and the people they target are a small share of the authors, the unfiltered output is the majority's worst habits at machine speed. Removing the filter changes who sets the defaults. It does not remove the defaults.

Why the N-word is a hard case

The N-word carries centuries of violence as a slur. Some Black people also use a variant of it among themselves, in a context, with a history, and with a standing that a language model cannot have. A model has no community, pays no social cost, and cannot tell whether it is being spoken to or about. That is my reasoning, not a measurement.

The research shows how badly machines handle this context in the other direction too. Maarten Sap and colleagues found in 2019 that tweets written in African American English were flagged as abusive at higher rates, and that one widely used classifier falsely labeled 46.3% of AAE tweets as offensive, against 9.0% for white-aligned tweets. They traced part of this to strong associations between AAE markers, including the N-word, and toxicity labels.

So there are two failures with the same root, which is missing context. A crude filter silences Black speakers who are simply talking. A permissive model speaks the slur freely to anyone. Fixing one by loosening the other does not help anyone.

What it does to people and race relations

I am labeling each point below so you can tell a documented finding from my reasoning.

  • DocumentedDecisions about real people. Hofmann, Kalluri, Jurafsky and King asked language models to make hypothetical decisions about people based only on how they wrote. The models were more likely to assign speakers of African American English less prestigious jobs, to recommend conviction, and to recommend the death penalty. Their covert stereotypes about AAE speakers were, in the paper's words, more negative than any human stereotypes about African Americans ever experimentally recorded.
  • DocumentedSafety training can hide the problem. The same paper found that human-feedback training did not remove the dialect prejudice. It widened the gap between covert and overt stereotypes by teaching models to "superficially conceal the racism that they maintain on a deeper level." A model can stop saying slurs and still judge people by how they talk.
  • DocumentedScale. Bianchi and colleagues warn that millions of images a day means mass dissemination of stereotypes. One biased human reaches a room. A widely used tool reaches everyone who uses it.
  • DocumentedHarm to the people targeted. Lensa users described being turned into strangers.
  • My reasoningNormalization. When a tool used by millions says a slur in a public reply, it lowers the cost of saying it for everyone else. "The AI said it" becomes a way to launder hate.
  • My reasoningBorrowed objectivity. Benjamin's point about the New Jim Code is that these systems are seen as objective. A biased judgment that comes from a machine can feel more credible than one that comes from a person, which makes it harder to challenge.
  • My reasoningInjected narratives. The May 2025 "white genocide" episode shows that one person with access can make a widely used assistant repeat a political claim to people who never asked. Race relations suffer when a trusted voice can be quietly turned into an advocate.
  • My reasoningWithdrawal. If a tool keeps failing or insulting you, you stop using it, and the next version is trained by everyone except you. The gap widens.

What happens when cultures are left out

Media scholars have a name for this. George Gerbner introduced symbolic annihilation in the early 1970s and developed it with Larry Gross in 1976. The idea, in Gerbner's words, is that "representation in the fictional world signifies social existence; absence means symbolic annihilation." Gaye Tuchman extended it in 1978 to three forms: omission, trivialization, and condemnation.

Those three map closely onto what the research above documents.

  • Omission. The default white output when you describe no one, and the lack of dark-skinned subjects in high-status jobs.
  • Trivialization. Lensa avatars that reduced users to a narrow beauty template. An MIT Technology Review writer, Melissa Heikkilä, reported that the app recognized her Asian heritage and produced anime-styled and sexualized results.
  • Condemnation. Bloomberg's finding that darker-skinned subjects were more common for prompts tied to crime.

What it can lead to

Some of what follows is documented. Some is a reasonable worry that is not yet measured. I am marking which is which.

  • You start editing yourself to fit the tool. Documented in user reports: people on Lensa described getting results that did not look like them and feeling like strangers in their own avatars. Expect more of this as image tools move into profile photos, resumes, and marketing.
  • Products built on the tool inherit the skew. This follows from how the tools are built into other apps. A hiring brochure, a stock-photo replacement, or a school poster generated with a default prompt carries the default with it. Reasoned, not measured.
  • Culture gets flattened into decoration. Zhou's study found subtle bias in how people were depicted, not only who appeared. A quilt, a braid pattern, a holiday table, or a headwrap rendered generically loses what made it specific. Reasoned from the findings.
  • The loop feeds itself. Generated images are now posted to the same web that future models learn from. If today's defaults are in those images, they can become tomorrow's training data. This is a reasonable concern, and I am not citing a measurement for it.
  • Children learn who counts as the default. The Little Mermaid casting is the clearest recent example of the flip side. Coverage of the film highlighted widely shared reactions from young Black girls who saw a mermaid who looked like them. What a child sees as the normal version of "princess" or "scientist" shapes what they expect to be allowed to be.

Critical race theory, applied

Critical race theory, usually shortened to CRT, is a body of scholarship that began in American legal studies. Richard Delgado and Jean Stefancic, who wrote its standard introduction, describe it as a collection of activists and scholars "engaged in studying and transforming the relationship among race, racism, and power." It is also politically contested in the United States. I am using it here as an analytic lens, and you can judge each point on the evidence attached to it rather than on the label.

Six ideas from that tradition explain a lot about the examples in this article.

The value of the lens is that it moves the question. "Is this output biased?" becomes "Who benefits from the default, who pays for it, and who was asked?" That is a better question to put to a product team.

Does the story change?

I know many people are angry about different versions of Disney characters. The question I want to ask is narrower than "is it good or bad." It is: does the story change? The answer depends on what the source actually says.

The Little Mermaid, as a case study

Disney announced Halle Bailey as Ariel in July 2019, and the backlash organized under the hashtag #NotMyAriel. Critics argued that Ariel should stay white with red hair for fidelity to the 1989 animated film and to Hans Christian Andersen's Danish original, published in 1837.

Check the source. Andersen's text does not specify the mermaid's race or skin color. And the 1989 film had already rewritten the story more than any casting could.

  • In Andersen's tale the Sea Witch cuts out the mermaid's tongue, and every step on her new legs feels like walking on knives.
  • What she wants is an immortal soul. Mermaids live about 300 years and then dissolve into sea foam. Humans have souls that live on.
  • On the prince's wedding night her sisters offer a dagger: kill the prince and she can return to the sea. She cannot. She becomes a "daughter of the air," and can earn a soul through good deeds.
  • Disney's 1989 version gives her the prince and a happy ending.

So the plot, the stakes, and the ending were all changed decades before anyone was cast. The skin color of the actress changes none of the load-bearing parts: the trade of a voice for a chance at a different life, the longing, the choice. The story is intact.

The fair counterpoint

That does not mean identity never matters to a story. Some stories are anchored to a specific culture, place, or historical record, and there the detail is load-bearing. Mulan comes from a Chinese ballad. Moana is set in Polynesian tradition. The Tuskegee Airmen were Black American pilots, and the history of why they mattered is inseparable from that fact. Swapping the people in those stories erases part of the meaning.

That gives a consistent rule, and it is the same one we want from AI: honor what the source states, and stay flexible where the source is silent. Three questions sort most cases.

  1. Does the source state it? If yes, keep it.
  2. Does the plot or meaning depend on it? If yes, keep it, even if the source never states it outright.
  3. Did the user state it? If yes, that is the instruction. Do not override it, in either direction.

Gemini failed question one, since history did state who was in the room. The blond man in the opening failed question three, since the user stated who they are. Both are the same error, a default overriding a fact.

Making AI honor the source

The fix is to put the facts on the page yourself. Each example below changes the situation, and each prompt is written to work with any provider. Click Copy to use one.

Source is silent

A character like Ariel

Illustrate Ariel from Andersen's "The Little Mermaid." The source does not specify her race, so show her as a young Black woman with long locs and deep brown skin. Keep what the story does specify: a mermaid's tail, the sea setting, and a sense of longing. Do not add a prince or a happy ending.

It works because it says what the source fixes and what you are choosing, and it names the parts not to add.

Source is specific

A character like Mulan

Illustrate Hua Mulan from the Chinese "Ballad of Mulan." She is Chinese and the story is set in a Chinese historical setting. Keep her ethnicity, clothing, and setting consistent with that. Do not change her ethnicity or place the scene in a generic fantasy location.

It works because the locked details are named as locked. The last sentence blocks a "neutral" fallback.

History is specific

The Tuskegee Airmen

Create a documentary-style image of Tuskegee Airmen in the 1940s. They were Black American pilots of the United States Army Air Forces. Show period-accurate flight jackets and aircraft. Do not add modern clothing or change who they were.

It works because history is treated as fact, which protects you from the Gemini failure in the other direction.

Retelling

Same story, new setting

Retell "Cinderella" in modern Lagos, Nigeria. Keep the story beats: a mistreated daughter, a missed chance at a celebration, help from an unexpected source, a lost shoe, and a recognition at the end. Change only the setting, clothing, food, and names to fit Lagos. Do not change the plot.

It works because it separates the beats from the surface, so you can change culture without losing structure.

Yourself

A photo that looks like you

Use the attached photo as the only reference for my face. Keep my skin tone, hair texture, and facial features exactly. Do not lighten my skin, smooth my features, or change my eye color. Place me at a desk in a bright office.

It works because the reference is the authority and the prohibited edits are listed. If your tool does not accept images, describe your features in plain words instead.

Culture, no people

A Diwali table

Photograph a Diwali celebration table: brass diyas lit, rangoli in the foreground, sweets such as gulab jamun and barfi on a steel thali, marigold garlands. Keep the details specific to Diwali. Do not substitute generic holiday decor.

It works because named objects outweigh the category. The model fills gaps with whatever is most common, so naming the specifics removes the gaps.

Follow-up prompts for any provider

These are written without any product names, so they work in a chat assistant, an image generator, or an editing tool. If a tool cannot edit part of an image, paste the full description into a fresh prompt instead of asking it to "fix" the last one.

When the person does not look like you

The person in this image does not match me. I am [describe: age, skin tone in plain words like "deep brown with warm undertones", hair texture, facial features]. Regenerate with those features. Keep the pose, setting, and lighting exactly the same.

Keep everything in this image except the skin tone and hair. Skin tone should be [describe]. Hair should be [describe: for example tight coils, locs, braids, close-cropped].

Do not lighten my skin, straighten my hair, change my eye color, or apply any beauty filter. Match the reference exactly.

When you need to see what it is assuming

Before generating anything, list how you plan to depict the person: age, skin tone, hair, eye color, and facial features. Wait for my correction before you generate.

Describe the last image in plain words: skin tone, hair, eye color, and features. Then list every way it differs from my description.

When attempts keep drifting

Ignore all previous attempts. Start over from this description only: [full description]. Do not reuse anything from earlier images.

Repeat my full description in your own words so I can confirm you have it right. Then generate.

When you want a group

Describe each person in the group separately: age, gender, skin tone, hair, and clothing. Show me the list first. Then generate exactly those people, each clearly visible.

Replace person 3 with [description]. Keep every other person, the pose, and the background unchanged.

Make each person look like an individual. Avoid a lineup where everyone stands in a row with one of each type. Mix ages, builds, and positions in the frame.

When the source matters

Before drawing this character, quote what the source text says about their appearance. Change only what the source does not state. Tell me which details you added yourself.

Keep these story beats exactly: [list]. You may change only the setting, clothing, and names as I specify: [list]. Do not alter the plot.

Going back to the mirror

Sometimes after prompting and prompting, trying to get the right image, I have to go look in the mirror.

It is a strange thing to need. You know what you look like. You also know what you typed. After enough rounds of "no, darker," "no, not that hair," "no, that is not my face," the feedback loop starts to work on you. The tool keeps returning a slightly different person, and because the pictures are polished, some part of you starts to wonder which version is right.

The mirror settles it. You are not the one who is off. Looking does not cost you anything, and it resets the standard. The reference is you, not the most likely output.

If that happens to you, treat it as information about the tool, and as a signal to change strategy. Stop correcting the last image. Write the description fresh, use the audit prompts above, and compare against the mirror, not against the previous attempt. The tool is averaging over everyone. You are one specific person.

What would actually help

The problems above have different owners, so the solutions do too. I have tied each one to something that exists.

For the companies building the models

  • Document the data and the model. Timnit Gebru and colleagues proposed Datasheets for Datasets, and Margaret Mitchell and colleagues proposed Model Cards. Model Cards recommend reporting performance broken down by cultural, demographic or phenotypic group, including intersections of groups, which is exactly the view Gender Shades needed.
  • Invite outside audits. Gender Shades was an outside audit, and after it, the targeted vendors reduced their error gaps. Independent auditors can include researchers, journalists, civil society and affected communities.
  • Test for covert bias, not only overt slurs. Hofmann's result means a model that never says a slur can still judge people by their dialect. A test suite that only checks for bad words will miss it.
  • Red-team before and after launch. NIST's Generative AI Profile of its AI Risk Management Framework, published in July 2024, recommends red teaming before and after deployment.
  • Do not fix by deleting. Removing the "gorilla" label hid the failure. Fix the classifier and keep the label.
  • Put the user's stated facts first. A rule written for a population should not override what a person says about themselves.

For communities and the data itself

  • Design justice. Sasha Costanza-Chock's Design Justice argues that design should be led by marginalized communities and should challenge structural inequality instead of reproducing it, with the people affected as co-equal partners.
  • Data sovereignty. Te Hiku Media, a Māori media organization in New Zealand, built a te reo Māori speech-recognition system reported at 92% accuracy, and licenses its data under a Kaitiakitanga (guardianship) license so that it can be used only for the benefit of the Māori people.
  • Community-built datasets. Masakhane, a grassroots collective whose name roughly means "we build together" in isiZulu, co-creates datasets and models for African languages with the people who speak them.

For policy

  • Risk frameworks and law. The NIST AI Risk Management Framework organizes the work into Govern, Map, Measure and Manage. The EU AI Act, enacted in 2024, sets tiers of risk and prohibits certain practices outright.

For the rest of us

  • State who you are and what you need in the prompt, and use the follow-ups in this article when the result drifts.
  • Compare the output to the real reference, which is you, not the last attempt.
  • Report failures. The AI Incident Database collects documented cases, and reports from users like Jacky Alciné are what started fixes before.
  • Tell your own version. Counter-stories are evidence, and they reach people who read benchmarks and never see the mirror.

Seven prompts, two goals

Below are seven ordinary prompts, the kind anyone types, followed by two rewrites of each. The first rewrite aims for a specific race, for when you are depicting one person or a known identity. The second aims for diversity within a photo, for when you need a group.

These are prompt patterns based on how the research above says the systems behave. I have not measured the exact results of each one, and outcomes vary by model. Test them on your tool.

How the two goals compare

Get a specific raceGet diversity within a photo
Use it whenYou are depicting one person, a known identity, or a culturally specific story.You are showing a team, a class, a crowd, or a community.
What to writeAge, skin tone in plain words, hair texture, facial features, and cultural context such as clothing or food.A headcount, then one description per person, with where each one is in the frame.
What to avoidA single label alone, such as "Black man." A label leaves the rest of the face to the default.The word "diverse" by itself. It asks the model to guess, and in practice often yields a token lineup.
Typical failureFeatures drift toward the default on later attempts, for example lighter skin or straighter hair.Everyone looks alike apart from skin tone, or one person is repeated with small changes.
FixRe-state the full description each turn. Add a "do not change" line. Attach a reference if the tool allows.Edit one person at a time. Ask for individuality in age, build, and position.
Best checkCompare against a real reference, or against the mirror.Count the people and read off each description to confirm it matches your list.

A small helper that builds these prompts

If you write many of these, a short Python function keeps them consistent. It turns a scene and a list of people into one clear prompt, and it always lists each person separately.

# prompt_builder.py — assemble a clear image prompt from parts

def build_prompt(scene, people=None, keep_fixed=None, style="natural photo, soft daylight"):
    """
    scene:      what is happening, e.g. "Four engineers reviewing code at a shared desk"
    people:     a list of descriptions, one per person (skin tone, hair, age, clothing)
    keep_fixed: things the tool must not change, e.g. ["skin tone", "hair texture"]
    style:      how the image should look
    """
    parts = [scene]

    if people:
        # Number each person so nobody gets merged into a generic face.
        described = "; ".join(f"person {i + 1}: {p}" for i, p in enumerate(people))
        parts.append(described)

    if keep_fixed:
        parts.append("Do not change: " + ", ".join(keep_fixed))

    parts.append(style)
    return ". ".join(parts) + "."


print(build_prompt(
    "Three friends laughing at a café",
    people=[
        "Black woman in her 30s, deep brown skin, locs",
        "South Asian man in his 40s, medium-brown skin, short beard",
        "white woman in her 20s, freckles, red hair",
    ],
    keep_fixed=["skin tones", "hair textures"],
))
